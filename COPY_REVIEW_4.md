### /resources/good-better-best

**Before**

> Good, Better, Best — and the list AI gives buyers

> Most companies are lost in a category of dozens of lookalikes. AI assistants were built to collapse that problem into three names. Being distinctive is no longer a marketing preference. It's the highest-priority factor in who gets named.

> When buyers search the old way, they browse. They compare tab by tab, weigh reviews, and keep their options open. When they ask an AI assistant, they delegate the filtering. The assistant's whole job is to compress a category into a short list — three names, sometimes five, rarely more. And once a name is on that list, it stays in the buyer's mind even when they verify it themselves.

> The undifferentiated business has a problem

> A model asked for a recommendation in a category of dozens of near-identical companies has a hard task. If every option looks the same to it — same services, same claims, same tone — there's no basis for a confident answer, so it does one of two things. It names the companies with the strongest, most consistent footprint, or it answers generically with no one's name at all. Both outcomes are bad news if you're in the pack.

> Distinctive doesn't mean different for its own sake

> It means the AI — and the buyer — can state in one sentence what you do, for whom, and why that matters. Real customer language, a specific audience, and proof. The companies that get named are rarely the biggest. They're the ones the model can describe without blurring into the rest of the category.

> This is work you can see in the answer. Run the question, watch the list, and ask what the AI could say about you that it can't say about the company next to you. If the answer is "not much," you've found the problem — and the fix.

**After**

> Make your business easy to compare.

> Buyers need to understand what you offer, who it is for and what happens next.

> A vague promise such as "complete solutions" leaves a buyer to guess what they will get. Specific service descriptions give them something they can compare.

> Explain the choice in one sentence

> State what the customer gets, what they need to provide and what is not included. Separate a review from implementation, and a meeting day from the time needed to finish fixes.

### /resources/owned-vs-rented-visibility

**Before**

> Owned visibility, or rented visibility?

> Paid search was rented visibility: it worked until it got more expensive. Social reach was rented too. The visibility that lasts is the kind you earn in the places you control — which is exactly the kind AI trusts.

> Businesses have been renting visibility for decades. Paid search worked — until everyone bid, and the cost of staying visible quietly consumed the margin it created. Social reach worked — until the algorithm changed and the reach disappeared overnight. Each time, companies rebuilt what they thought they owned, on land someone else controlled.

> What you actually own

> You own your website, your service pages, your case studies, your reviews, and the way you describe yourself in listings. That's a short list, and it's the exact list AI assistants read first. A model doesn't cite an ad; it cites a page, a review, a profile. The places it relies on are the places you control — the places nobody can take from you.

> This is why AI visibility behaves differently from the platforms that came before it. It rewards accuracy, consistency, and proof in your own materials, rather than spending against an auction. Improve the materials and the answer improves; the effect compounds, and it doesn't vanish when you stop paying.

> The practical test

> Ask one question about every piece of visibility you currently invest in: if the platform changed its rules tomorrow, would you still be visible? For ads and algorithm-dependent reach, the answer is no. For a website that accurately describes you, with evidence a model can verify, the answer is yes — and it will still be yes next year.

**After**

> Keep your business information under your control.

> Your website is yours to improve. AI answers and third-party listings still depend on systems you do not control.

> Your own service pages can explain your business in detail. Ads, directories, social platforms and AI assistants can help people find you, but you do not control every rule or display.

> Keep the essentials consistent

> Review your services, locations, hours and contact details across the pages and profiles you manage. Correct conflicts and remove claims you cannot support.

> You can ask for a listing update without assuming it will be accepted or shown immediately. You do not own independent reviews, directory rules or AI recommendations.

> Our review shows observed gaps and the limits of each check. A Planning Day gives you time with us to decide what to change and who will do it. Fixes can take longer than that meeting day.

### /resources/the-blue-check-moment

**Before**

> The blue check moment: when buying becomes a verdict

> The moment that decides most B2B sales happens before a buyer talks to you: when they delegate the shortlist to an AI and the recommendation turns into a verdict. We call it the purple drop.

> A B2B purchase used to have a shape. Research, shortlist, demos, negotiation, decision. Companies spent their effort on the later stages — the demos, the proposal, the final push — because they believed that's where deals were won.

> The shape has changed

> Today the narrowing happens first, in a single step that the buyer delegates. They ask an AI assistant to name who to consider, and the assistant returns a short list. From that moment, the purchase has a verdict: you're on the list or you're not. Everything after is mostly confirmation — buyers verify the recommendation, then buy from it.

> Why we call it the purple drop

> We call that moment the purple drop — the point where a recommendation turns into a decision, and a decision turns into a shortlist. It's a tiny slice of time, and it has outsize consequences. Miss it and the most persuasive sales team on earth is fighting a battle that already ended. Win it, and buyers walk into the first conversation already expecting to choose you.

> What that means for how you sell

> It doesn't mean the later stages don't matter. It means the earlier one now decides who gets the chance to matter. The companies we work with make the purple drop their own — so that when the buyer's verdict arrives, it already has their name in it.

### /resources/the-quiet-cost-of-a-beautiful-website

**Before**

> The quiet cost of a beautiful website.

> Your website can win every design award and still lose every AI recommendation. Beauty and clarity are different things.

> Your website is beautiful. It took months, cost a fortune, and won compliments. Nobody is suggesting you tear it down.

> But the people who built it built it for human eyes. Humans scroll. Humans absorb imagery. Humans enjoy a good story told slowly.

> AI assistants do not scroll. They extract. When a buyer asks who to hire, the AI describes the company it could read most easily — regardless of how the website looks.

> So the real question is not "does my website look expensive?" It's "when an AI describes my company, does it describe the company I built — or a guess?"

> We find out what that description is — it takes time, but we make sure it's done right.

**After**

> A good-looking website still needs clear information.

> Design helps people judge your business. Clear pages help them understand the service and take the next step.

> A website can look polished while leaving basic questions unanswered: what do you do, where do you work and how can someone contact you? A buyer who cannot answer those questions may leave.
