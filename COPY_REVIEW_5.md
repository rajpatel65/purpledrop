### /resources/what-ai-is-skeptical-of

**Before**

> What AI is skeptical of — and what it trusts

> Models are professionally suspicious. They've been trained on a web full of claims, so they look for the same evidence a careful buyer would: real customer language, verifiable proof, and plain English.

> There's a misconception that AI assistants believe what they read. They don't. They were trained on the whole web — including the parts that exaggerate — and they learned to be cautious. When a model describes a company, it's effectively running a credibility check: does this claim have support, and does the source material agree?

> Three things AI is skeptical of

> Unsupported claims. "Best-in-class," "trusted by thousands," "industry leading" — a model hears these constantly and weighs them at zero. There's no date, no number, no name behind them. They make you harder to recommend, not easier.

> Generic language. Copy that could belong to any company in the category tells a model nothing it can use to distinguish you — and a model needs a reason to name one company over another. If it can't find one, it leaves you out.

> Stale content. AI systems weigh currency. A website that hasn't changed in years, an abandoned blog, a LinkedIn profile that stops after 2019 — these quietly lower the confidence a model has in everything else about you.

> What it trusts instead

> The same things a careful human buyer trusts. Real customer language — how actual clients describe the problem you solve, not how marketers do. Verifiable proof — dates, numbers, named work, measurable outcomes. And plain English, because the more directly your materials state who you are and what you do, the less room there is for a model to describe you wrong.

> This is the whole method, and it's why it works: you don't have to trick the AI. You have to make the sources it reads say the true thing about you — out loud, in plain language, with the proof to back it.

**After**

> Use proof you can check, not claims you cannot.

> Clear facts help a buyer evaluate your business. They do not let us promise what an AI tool will believe.

> "Best in the market" is a claim. A dated case study explaining a real problem, the work and the measured result gives a buyer more to evaluate. Publish only details you can support and have permission to share.

> Check your own claims

> Look for numbers without sources, outdated details and customer names used without permission. Remove unsupported claims instead of dressing them up with stronger language.

> Be clear about limits

> Do not call a public security check a certification. Do not call a brand mention a sale. Do not treat an AI answer as verified just because it sounds confident.

> Our review separates observations, uncertainties and next steps. The aim is useful decisions about your website and listings, not a story about knowing every tool's hidden rules.

### /resources/who-does-ai-recommend

**Before**

> Who does AI actually recommend — and why?

> When a buyer asks an AI assistant for a recommendation, it doesn't pick the best company. It picks the one it can understand most easily. That's a different list — and it can be changed.

> Ask any owner about their competitors and you'll get a story about quality, price, or longevity. Ask the same owner what an AI assistant says when a buyer asks who to call, and you'll usually get a shrug. The gap between those two answers is the whole market.

> Here's what the people building these systems will tell you, if you press: a model doesn't rank businesses by who is best. It has no ground truth. It reads everything available about a company and produces the answer that is most consistent, most specific, and most supported by the sources it trusts.

> Three inputs decide the answer

> First, clarity. If your website, reviews, and listings say the same thing in plain language, the model can confidently describe you. If your copy is vague — "premium solutions," "best-in-class service" — the model has nothing to quote and moves on to someone more concrete.

> Second, consistency. Models weigh sources that agree with each other. When your Google profile, your site, your LinkedIn, and your reviews all tell the same story, that story becomes the truth. When they contradict each other, the model hedges — and hedged answers don't include you by name.

> Third, proof. A claim with evidence is a fact. A claim without it is a guess. Reviews, case studies, dates, named clients, measurable outcomes — these are the things a model can verify and therefore repeat. The more of them it finds, the more confidently it recommends you.

> Why this is good news

> If the answer were based on being genuinely best, you couldn't do anything about it quickly. But it's based on being understood, consistent, and provable — which is work you can actually do. Every source you clean up, every claim you back with evidence, moves the answer in your direction.

> The first step isn't guesswork either. Run the search yourself: ask three assistants the question a buyer would ask, and look at who appears and who doesn't. That's your starting line. We run that review for clients during a consulting day — and most are surprised by how much they were missing.

**After**

> Who does AI recommend? Check the answer.

> There is no single public rule that decides every AI recommendation. Start with evidence you can inspect.

> Different tools and questions can produce different business recommendations. A confident-sounding answer is not proof that the businesses named are the best, or that yours was checked and rejected.

> Record the answer before explaining it

> Keep the question, location, tool, date, answer and linked sources. Check whether your name appears, whether the facts are right and which other businesses are mentioned.

> Improve what you can control

> Your service pages and business details should be accurate and specific. Check for conflicting hours, locations or contact details. Back claims with evidence you can publish with permission.

### /resources/you-are-not-in-the-answer

**Before**

> You're not in the answer. Nobody told you.

> The most expensive sentence your competitors will never see: when a buyer asks AI who to call, and your company simply isn't there. You didn't lose the sale. You never knew the question was asked.

> A company can spend years building a reputation and still be invisible in the place where decisions are now being made. Not wrong, not disliked — absent. When a buyer asks an AI assistant "who's the best X for a company like ours," a list appears, and it doesn't include you. Nobody emails you about it. You never find out the question was asked.

> The silence is the expensive part

> In a normal sales situation, losing a deal costs you time, but it costs you nothing else. You know it happened. You can learn from it. Being absent from an AI answer is the opposite: the cost is invisible, recurring, and compounding. Every week, some number of buyers ask about your category, get a list, and move on. There's no feedback loop, so there's no urgency — until you run the search and see it yourself.

> The first time most companies see the actual answer, the reaction is the same. It's not anger at the AI. It's confusion that the gap between their reputation and their digital footprint got this wide without anyone noticing.

> Why it happens to good companies

> It's almost never because the business is bad. It's because the materials AI reads — website, profiles, listings, reviews — were written for a human visitor, and they reward different things than a model does. Vague slogans, missing service pages, no dates, no named clients, thin local listings. A human reads between the lines; a model quotes what's there.

> The fix is not to chase the AI. It's to make the materials you already own accurate enough that any careful reader — human or model — describes you the way your best customers would. That's the entire job, and it's a lot more predictable than it sounds.

**After**

> Missing from an answer can mean missing an enquiry.

> The risk is real enough to check. The size of the loss is not something a scan can tell you.

> When a buyer sees other businesses and not yours, they may contact someone else. That is a reason to investigate your visibility, not permission to invent a monthly loss figure.

> Find the gap

> Test questions relevant to your services and location. Check whether your business is named and whether the contact details and description are correct. Keep the evidence so a later check can be compared.

> Do not confuse a gap with a lost sale

> An omitted name in a test does not prove a buyer saw that answer, would have chosen you or would have paid a particular amount. Sales records and customer feedback are needed to investigate leads.

> Use the lead value calculator with your own numbers if you want to explore a scenario. Use the paid review to decide what needs attention. Neither guarantees more enquiries.
